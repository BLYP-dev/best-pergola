The short answer: **most pergolas in England and Wales don't need planning permission** — they fall under Permitted Development rights. But the rules hinge on specifics, and getting them wrong is expensive.

## Permitted Development rules for pergolas

Under Class E of Schedule 2 to the Town and Country Planning (General Permitted Development) (England) Order 2015, garden structures are Permitted Development if they meet all of the following:

- **Height** — no more than 2.5 m within 2 m of a boundary; up to 3.5 m further away (4 m for a dual-pitched roof)
- **Position** — located in the curtilage of the dwelling, behind the rear wall of the original house
- **Coverage** — the structure, together with any existing outbuildings, must not cover more than 50% of the garden

If all three conditions are met, you don't need to apply for planning permission.

## When you do need permission

The Permitted Development rules don't apply if:

- Your property is a **listed building** — any works require Listed Building Consent
- You're in a **conservation area**, National Park, or Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty — the same rules apply on paper, but local councils often have Article 4 directions that remove PD rights
- The pergola is at the **front or side** of the house (not rear)
- Your pergola is attached to the house and could be considered an extension

## Attached pergolas vs freestanding

An attached pergola that is structurally connected to the house is treated differently — it may be assessed as a house extension under Class A rather than a garden structure under Class E. The height rules are stricter and the 50% coverage calculation works differently.

## Getting certainty

If you're unsure, apply for a **Lawful Development Certificate** (LDC) from your local planning authority. It costs around £206 in England (as of 2026) and gives you legal confirmation that no planning permission is needed. It's not mandatory, but it protects you if you ever sell.
